\subsection*{Comment R1-2: ``RF is optimal'' is hurt by the absence of a statistically significant difference between RF and Chemprop}

\end{reviewer}

\response{We agree. We have removed every instance of ``RF is optimal'' or equivalent language and replaced it with statements that explicitly acknowledge the statistical equivalence between RF and the D-MPNN. After adopting the Greenman/Song duplicate-handling protocol (in response to reviewer concern R1-8) and retraining all five model families on the resulting v3 cleaned dataset, the RF--Chemprop paired $t$-test $p$-value moved from $0.77$ on the original v2 first-occurrence-dedup data to $0.16$ on v3; in both cases the null hypothesis of equal mean RMSE cannot be rejected at $\alpha = 0.05$, so the statistical-tie language is preserved. We are explicit in the manuscript about the v2-to-v3 shift and note that the entire RF nominal advantage on v3 is driven by a single Chemprop fold (fold~4, RMSE = 39.21~nm) whose early-stopping behaviour produced an outlier; Chemprop's fold-to-fold standard deviation on v3 (3.27) is comparable to its v2 std (3.18). \textbf{[Amended in light of R1-12.]} In response to reviewer R1-12 we subsequently ran a budget-matched 25-trial Optuna probe of Chemprop's hyperparameters and retrained the best configuration on all five v3 folds; the tuned Chemprop achieves $28.83 \pm 1.32$~nm RMSE (a $4.32$~nm mean improvement over default, paired-$t$ $p = 0.074$, Wilcoxon $p = 0.125$), with the fold-4 outlier eliminated (39.21 $\to$ 28.46~nm) and the standard deviation reduced from $3.27$ to $1.32$. The tuned-vs-RF paired comparison gives $\Delta = -2.67$~nm with a parametric $p = 0.044$ but a non-parametric Wilcoxon $p = 0.125$ (four of the five paired differences favour tuned Chemprop, the fifth favours RF by $0.37$~nm; the minimum achievable Wilcoxon $p$ for $n = 5$ is $0.0625$). The substantial overall novel-subset RMSE (50--57~nm for all four local-feature models) is a separate matter discussed in the Limitations section and in the response to R1-9.}

\subsection*{Comment R1-9: 64/16/20 split rationale and chromophore-solvent leakage}
\begin{reviewer}
Why was 64/16/20 chosen as the split fractions? These numbers are a bit unusual --- did these simply fall out of the solvent stratification process? Does this splitting strategy address the data leakage concerns raised by prior work (\url{https://doi.org/10.1039/d1sc05677h}) for the same chromophore appearing in both train and test sets in different solvents?
\end{reviewer}

\response{The reviewer's question has two parts and we address both.

\textbf{(a) 64/16/20 origin.} The reviewer is correct that these fractions are not chosen arbitrarily, they fall out of a nested-split design. We use a stratified 5-fold outer split (80\% train+val / 20\% test per fold, stratified on solvent identity to ensure rare-solvent coverage in every test fold), composed with an inner \texttt{train\_test\_split} of the trainval portion (80\% train / 20\% val of trainval). Combining these gives 64\% train / 16\% val / 20\% test of the total. The deep learning models (BiGRU, Chemprop, ChemBERTa) use the val slice for early stopping; the fingerprint-based models (RF, XGBoost) ignore the val slice and fit on the 64\% train portion only. We have added a sentence to the Methods Evaluation Protocol making this nested-split origin explicit.

\textbf{(b) Chromophore-solvent leakage.} The reviewer is also correct that the standard stratified-CV protocol does \emph{not} address the chromophore-overlap concern Greenman et al.\ raised. We audited our v3 5-fold splits and found a substantial leakage rate: 66.8\% of test records have their canonical solute also appear in the train slice under a different solvent (69.3\% if the val slice is counted). The corresponding leak rate at the unique-solute level is 54.6\% per fold. The reviewer's concern is therefore not just theoretically valid for our protocol, it is materially present.

Table~S18 in the revised Supporting Information (new Section~S11) decomposes each model's test-fold RMSE into chromophore-seen and chromophore-novel subsets. The decomposition is striking: the chromophore-seen subset gives RMSEs of 16--45~nm, the chromophore-novel subset gives 50--70~nm, and the $\Delta$RMSE (novel minus seen) is $+25$ to $+36$~nm across all five model families. We summarise the key reading of this analysis as follows.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
 \item \textbf{All models are affected comparably.} The 25--36~nm spread of $\Delta$RMSE is narrow relative to the size of the effect itself. In particular, RF and Chemprop have $\Delta$RMSE of $+33.16$ and $+32.26$~nm respectively. A paired $t$-test on the chromophore-novel subset alone (n~=~5 folds) gives $t = -2.51$, $p = 0.066$ (still failing to reject the null at $\alpha = 0.05$) so the RF$\approx$Chemprop tie ($p = 0.16$ on the full v3 test set) is preserved when the comparison is restricted to chromophores genuinely absent from the training data.

The wetlab validation set (16 commercial UV-absorbers, of which 15 are absent from the training data at the SMILES level \textbf{[Qualifier added per R1-13: although 15/16 of the same molecules share a Bemis--Murcko generic scaffold with training, so the wetlab tests molecule-level rather than scaffold-class generalisation; see new SI Section~S15 and the R1-13 response]}) and the Jung 2024 external dataset ($\sim$50\% solute overlap, separate experimental provenance) are the more rigorous out-of-distribution complements; both are already in the manuscript.
  \item \textbf{The magnitude is intrinsic to the dataset shape.} With 7{,}846 unique solutes and 18{,}415 records, the dataset averages $\sim$2.35 records per chromophore, so any random or solvent-stratified split will produce high chromophore-level overlap by construction. A truly novel-chromophore evaluation requires scaffold splitting, which we address separately in R1-13.
\end{itemize}

The complete analysis is reproducible from the saved v3 per-fold predictions via \texttt{python3 analyze\_chromophore\_leakage.py}, which writes \texttt{results/r1\_9\_leakage\_\{stats.json,per\_model.csv,per\_fold.csv,summary.md\}}. The leakage script is distributed with the code.}

\subsection*{Comment R1-16: ChemBERTa $\sim$600~nm prediction ceiling explanation}
\begin{reviewer}
The behavior of ChemBERTa shown in the parity plot in Figure 5 is strange for high wavelengths. This seems quite unusual to have what appears to be a ``ceiling'' where many compounds that have experimental wavelengths above 600 are all predicted to be about 600nm. This seems to be the reason behind the very high RMSE for ChemBERTa due to outliers while the MAE remains at a more expected level. Do the authors have an explanation for this behavior?
\end{reviewer}

\response{We diagnosed this with a three-hypothesis investigation reported in new Supporting Information Section~S21. The key finding is that ChemBERTa's predictions \emph{never exceed $556.4$~nm} (a hard ceiling, not gradual degradation) even though the v3 training distribution extends to $1{,}100$~nm; on the $1{,}555$ test records with experimental $\lambda_{\max} > 600$~nm, $98.5$\% are underpredicted by $\geq 50$~nm (median underprediction $= 126$~nm).

\textbf{Hypothesis testing:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{(H1) Training-target imbalance, supported.} Fold-0 train contains only $8.3$\% of records with $\lambda_{\max} > 600$~nm, $2.3$\% with $> 700$~nm, and $0.4$\% with $> 800$~nm. The deep-coloured chromophores are a long thin tail in the training distribution.
  \item \textbf{(H2) Output-range constraint, supported.} Max ChemBERTa prediction = $556$~nm regardless of input. With target normalisation (mean $= 0$, std $= 1$ during training) and MAE loss, the regression head minimises pooled MAE by regressing toward the training mean for inputs it cannot discriminate, and the maximum achievable output is bounded by the magnitudes of [CLS] activations the model has learned to produce.
  \item \textbf{(H3) SMILES-length truncation, ruled out.} Only $0.23$\% of test records exceed the MAX\_LEN $= 256$ token cap. Mean SMILES length increases from $61$ chars at $\lambda_{\max} < 400$~nm to $102$ chars at $\lambda_{\max} > 700$~nm, but the truncation rate per bin is uniformly $\leq 0.5$\%. Truncation is not the dominant cause.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Diagnosis.} The proximate cause is the regression head's output range (H2). The deeper cause is the pretraining-representation mismatch: ChemBERTa was pretrained on ZINC (\texttt{seyonec/ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1}), whose drug-like molecules are dominated by short-wavelength absorbers; the [CLS] embeddings ChemBERTa produces for long-wavelength chromophores (extended polyenes, push--pull cyanines, BODIPYs, porphyrins) are not well-separated in the pretrained representation from those of medium-wavelength absorbers, and the regression head fine-tuned on top of these collapsed embeddings cannot push its predictions beyond the $\sim$556~nm boundary even when shown long-wavelength training targets, because the input representations are not sufficiently distinct. Training-target imbalance (H1) compounds this. The remedy is either to pretrain on a UV-Vis-rich corpus (a different pretraining task than ZINC's drug-likeness) or to augment v3 with long-wavelength chromophores.

We have added (a) a paragraph titled ``ChemBERTa's $\sim$556~nm prediction ceiling'' to the Error Analysis subsection in the main text with the headline numbers and the three-factor diagnosis, (b) a new Limitations bullet warning practitioners not to rely on ChemBERTa's point predictions for deep-coloured chromophores, and (c) new SI Section~S21 with the full three-hypothesis investigation, a three-panel diagnostic figure (train/test distributions, parity colour-coded by SMILES truncation, SMILES length by $\lambda_{\max}$ bin), and the per-bin truncation table.}
